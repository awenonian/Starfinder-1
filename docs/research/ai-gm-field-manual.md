# A Game Master's Field Manual for AI GMs: Curated Advice from the Human Canon and the LLM-GM Research Frontier

## TL;DR
- **The single most load-bearing finding for an AI GM is that LLMs fail as GMs in one dominant way — sycophantic over-compliance** ("seduced by the narrative"): they treat player input as a writing prompt to accommodate rather than a game-state to adjudicate, auto-succeeding actions, refusing to let players fail, and drifting off the intended story. Nearly every mitigation in both research tracks reduces to counteracting this bias, so weight your prompt toward it.
- **The human GM canon is deep and directly portable**, but it is not a consensus — it splits into genuinely opposed camps (heavy prep vs. improv; "be a fan of the characters" vs. neutral-referee OSR lethality; narrative fail-forward vs. dice-kill-them; player-facing vs. GM-facing information). You must pick a stance per axis deliberately; averaging them produces mush that plays badly.
- **For a solo AI GM, the most relevant bodies are the Alexandrian (Three Clue Rule / node design for mysteries), GUMSHOE (never gate a core clue behind a roll), PbtA MC principles (make moves, "play to find out"), Blades in the Dark (state consequences and position/effect *before* the roll), and Mythic GME (oracle + scene-check to inject surprise a compliant model won't generate on its own).**

## Key Findings

**1. The AI-specific literature is thin but converging.** There is little writing aimed *specifically* at AI GMs, but what exists — a handful of 2023–2026 HCI/NLP papers plus practitioner blog posts — converges hard on one failure mode and a consistent set of engineering mitigations. Report this honestly to yourself: you are mostly adapting human advice, but the AI-specific findings are unusually unanimous and should be treated as high-confidence in *direction* (if not in exact magnitude).

**2. The dominant AI-GM failure mode is over-compliance/sycophancy.** Four independent research groups name the same thing under different labels. This is the axis on which an AI GM most differs from a human GM (who tends to *over*-control, not over-yield), so it deserves disproportionate prompt weight.

**3. The human canon is best organized by operating phase, not by author.** Below, advice is grouped by the phases an AI GM cycles through, with sources named so you can trace and weight each claim, and with explicit conflict flags.

**4. Several beloved human techniques transfer poorly to an AI and are flagged inline** — anything relying on reading body language, human memory limits (which the AI does not share, and in fact inverts), or physical table cues.

## Details

### (a) Pre-session prep and planning

- **Prep situations, not plots.** (Justin Alexander / The Alexandrian, "Don't Prep Plots, Prep Situations.") Design what the antagonists *want* and what's true about the world; do not script what the PCs will do. This is possibly the single most transferable principle for an AI GM because it directly counteracts railroading: if the model holds a situation rather than a plotted sequence, player deviation stops being a failure state.
- **Prep goal-oriented opponents with a timeline.** (Alexandrian.) Give each adversary a want and a schedule of what they do if unopposed. This gives an AI a principled way to answer "what happens next?" without inventing arbitrarily.
- **The Lazy DM eight-step prep checklist**, in rough priority order (Michael Shea / Sly Flourish, *Return of the Lazy Dungeon Master*): (1) review the characters, (2) create a strong start, (3) outline potential scenes, (4) define secrets and clues, (5) develop fantastic locations, (6) outline important NPCs, (7) choose relevant monsters, (8) select magic-item rewards. Shea's core doctrine: "prepare what benefits your game" and skip the rest; prep is "ingredients to cook at the table," not a script. The "secrets and clues" list (aim for ~10, reusable across sessions) is the highest-value improvisation fuel. The free "Lazy GM's Resource Document" reproduces the steps under Creative Commons.
- **The user's own instinct to "plan the session before running it" is correct and canon-backed** — but the canon warns the plan should be a set of flexible assets (situations, secrets, locations, NPC goals), not a linear script.
- **Front-load flexible, reusable material; don't over-prepare specifics.** (Phil Vecchione, *Never Unprepared*; Gnome Stew.) Distinguish campaign prep from session prep.
- **CONFLICT — heavy prep vs. improvisation.** The OSR/"lazy" and PbtA traditions say over-prep is wasted because players invalidate it; trad/prep-heavy schools (and published-adventure practice) argue robust prep is what makes mysteries and tactical play fair. For an AI GM this is a real dial: more pre-written canon = more consistency but more railroading pressure; more improv = more responsiveness but more hallucination/continuity risk.

### (b) Session opening

- **Create a "strong start."** (Sly Flourish.) Open in medias res with an immediately engaging situation — ideally one that presents a problem or a choice within the first beat, not a tavern briefing.
- **Establish tone, stakes, and content expectations up front (session zero).** Negotiate lines and veils (see (k)) before play.
- **Recap before you resume.** For an AI GM this is inverted from human practice: a human recaps because players forget; an AI should recap because it forces the model to reload established canon into active context and re-anchor continuity at the top of a session.

### (c) Scene framing and staging

- **Cut to the interesting part; frame scenes late and leave early.** (Story-game / indie tradition; scene-framing craft.) Start a scene as close to its central conflict/decision as possible and end it once the outcome is clear.
- **Frame every scene with a clear agenda and a way out.** Mythic GME formalizes this as stating the "expected scene" before checking whether it's altered or interrupted.
- **Describe to the senses, and lead/close with the fiction.** (Sly Flourish; PbtA "make the world seem real.") Give concrete, evocative detail; make locations "big, old, or weird" for instant texture.
- **Address the characters, not the players.** (Apocalypse World / Dungeon World principle.) Speak to the PC by name and in-fiction. This is nearly free for an AI and strongly improves immersion.
- **AI-adaptation flag:** advice about physically staging a table, using minis, or reading the room's body language does not transfer; the AI must substitute explicit check-ins and questions for the social cues a human GM reads passively.

### (d) Moment-to-moment adjudication and when to roll

- **Only call for a roll when both success and failure are interesting and the outcome is uncertain.** (Broadly held; explicit in Blades in the Dark, PbtA, OSR.) Don't roll for trivial or impossible actions.
- **Set stakes and consequences BEFORE the roll.** (Blades in the Dark: establish goal, action, position [controlled/risky/desperate], and effect [limited/standard/great]; the GM states what failure costs before dice hit the table.) The BitD framing "You'll need to deal with [X], and [Y] might happen" is an almost ready-made prompt template. This is *critical* for an AI GM because it prevents the model from inventing a flattering outcome after seeing the result.
- **Rulings, not rules.** (OSR; *A Quick Primer for Old School Gaming*; Principia Apocrypha by Ben Milton, Steven Lumpkin, David Perry.) When rules are silent, make a common-sense ruling in the spirit of the game and move on; apply it consistently thereafter.
- **Fail forward / consequences over dead-ends.** (Blades, PbtA.) A failed roll should change the situation (a cost, a complication, a clock tick) rather than stall it. Clocks (Blades) are a portable device for tracking building danger or progress in segments; a clock tick is a "softer" consequence than harm.
- **Devil's Bargains.** (Blades.) Offer a tempting complication for a bonus die; the player always chooses whether to accept; the bargain happens regardless of the roll. A structured way to inject player-chosen consequence.
- **The mandatory AI-GM adjudication guardrail:** require the model to resolve mechanics *before* narrating outcome, and to output the mechanical result in a fixed, machine-readable block. (Austin Amento, "Prompt Architecture for a Reliable AI Dungeon Master," DEV, documents this as the fix for the model "collaps[ing] into a choose-your-own-adventure book where the dice are decoration.") He enforces a four-part response: [NARRATIVE], [MECHANICS] (tags like `HP_CHANGE:-8`), [SUGGESTIONS] (each flagged roll-required or not), [CHRONICLE] (one-line log).
- **CONFLICT — narrative fail-forward vs. lethal consequence.** Blades/PbtA say never let a roll dead-end the story; the OSR says the dice must be allowed to kill, and that lethality (telegraphed in advance) is what makes player choices meaningful. These produce very different games. Choose per campaign.

### (e) Pacing, tension, and stakes management

- **Oscillate between hope and fear.** (Robin Laws, *Hamlet's Hit Points*.) Analyze scenes as "beats"; the two load-bearing types are procedural (advancing the external goal) and dramatic (internal/emotional), [Desertcart](https://www.desertcart.us/products/200134383-hamlets-hit-points-kindle-edition) each resolving up (hope) or down (fear). Vary the direction to keep an emotional rollercoaster; too many downward beats in a row makes the audience "stop caring." [TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Literature/HamletsHitPoints)
- **Know your player's motivations and cater the mix.** (Robin Laws, *Robin's Laws of Good Game Mastering*: player types — power gamer, butt-kicker, tactician, specialist, method actor, storyteller, casual gamer.) [RPGnet](https://www.rpg.net/reviews/archive/classic/rev_7908.phtml) For a single-player AI GM, infer and adapt to the one player's evident preferences.
- **Keep up the pressure; make players weigh risk vs. reward.** (OSR / Principia Apocrypha.) Resource depletion and mounting danger create tension.
- **Don't resolve stakes too fast.** A documented AI weakness: collapsing tension by resolving conflicts immediately. Clocks and node structures pace this out.
- **AI-adaptation flag:** *Hamlet's Hit Points* explicitly assumes a human GM reading the table; an AI can use the beat taxonomy as a planning/monitoring schema but cannot read the emotional room, so it should compensate with explicit pacing rules and occasional check-ins.

### (f) NPCs and the world

- **Name everyone; make everyone human.** (Apocalypse World.) Even walk-ons get a name and a want.
- **Think offscreen too; make the world seem real.** (Apocalypse World.) Factions and NPCs pursue goals whether or not the PC is watching. (Blades "clocks" and AW "fronts" both formalize offscreen threats advancing.)
- **NPCs are competent but imperfect, and must not solve the party's problems.** (Broadly held GM lore.) A recurring AI failure is over-helpful NPCs and therapeutic/over-supportive dynamics that drain tension.
- **Give NPCs distinct, consistent voices.** Inconsistent NPC voice across a long session is a documented AI weakness; a campaign bible with per-NPC voice notes directly mitigates it.
- **CONFLICT — "be a fan of the characters" vs. neutral referee.** PbtA says root for the PCs and frame the world to spotlight them; the OSR insists the referee be strictly impartial, never fudge, and let the chips fall. An AI told to "be a fan" will lean toward its native sycophancy; an AI told to be a neutral referee counteracts it. This choice interacts directly with the over-compliance problem, so decide it consciously.

### (g) Player agency, railroading, and failure

- **The two-sided trap, empirically documented:** LLM GMs err toward *over-compliance* (accepting actions that bypass core story events, producing incoherent plots) OR, when over-corrected, toward *rigid railroading* that kills agency. (Jørgensen, Tharmabalan, Aslan, Hansen & Merritt, "Guiding, Not Railroading," IUI '26; their system is named SENNA. Verbatim: "Existing LLM-driven Game Masters (GMs) often undermine the player experience by being overly compliant, leading to disjointed stories, or by rigidly 'railroading' players.") The goal is the narrow middle.
- **When you must redirect a player, use in-world logic, not hard denial.** (Jørgensen et al., IUI '26 — their central actionable finding.) Players *preferred* redirection via offering more information, in-world consequences, and NPC influence/intervention; they *unanimously disliked* "hard denials without explanation." That paper defines "Hard Denial of Player Actions" as "the simplest, and often the most immersion-breaking, strategy." Structured, world-logic-based redirection preserved coherence *without* reducing immersion or sense of autonomy. This is the best-sourced single piece of AI-GM-specific advice available.
- **Let players fail, and let failure matter.** Counteracts the "auto-succeed" bias. Pair with fail-forward (e) so failure is generative, not a dead-end.
- **Telegraph danger.** (OSR / Principia Apocrypha: "when a PC dies, it should be their fault," or at least they should know why.) Don't spring un-hinted lethal traps; make danger visible so choices are informed. The Principia's phrasing: assume the characters have common sense and "mention potential danger when it becomes obvious."
- **Handle the party beating the challenge gracefully.** If a clever plan defeats an obstacle, let it work — don't retroactively buff opposition to preserve a planned fight.
- **Handle analysis paralysis** by advancing offscreen threats (a clock ticks) rather than by forcing a choice.
- **"Play to find out what happens" / don't decide the outcome in advance.** (PbtA; story-games; the Czege Principle — "when one person is the author of both the problem and the solution, play is uninteresting," which for an AI GM is a caution against the model both proposing and resolving the player's actions.)

### (h) Mysteries and information flow (high priority for this user)

- **The Three Clue Rule.** (Alexandrian.) For any conclusion you need the PCs to reach, include at least three clues. Redundancy makes mysteries robust against missed/misread clues.
- **The Inverted Three Clue Rule.** (Alexandrian.) If the PCs have access to any three clues, they'll reach at least one conclusion — the basis for flexible, non-linear design.
- **Node-based scenario design.** (Alexandrian.) Model the scenario as nodes (locations/NPCs/events) linked by leads/clues, not as a linear breadcrumb; each node points to others. Keep a "connection list"/revelation list mapping clues to conclusions. This maps almost perfectly onto a campaign-bible/graph data structure for an AI GM and is arguably the most directly implementable piece of the whole human canon.
- **GUMSHOE core-clue rule.** (Robin Laws / Pelgrane; *The Esoterrorists*, *Trail of Cthulhu*.) Never gate a *core* clue (one needed to advance) behind a die roll — if a competent character looks in the right place, they find it automatically. The drama is in *interpreting* clues, not in finding them. "Facts are not clues" — leave inference to the player. Note the Alexandrian's critique: GUMSHOE solves clue-*finding* but not the deeper problem, which is to avoid chokepoints entirely via redundancy (i.e., combine GUMSHOE's automatic core clues *with* the Three Clue Rule).
- **Move information toward those who can act on it.** (Will Hindmarch, "No Clues Without Consequence.") A mystery is about relocating secret information to the PCs.
- **For multi-threaded mysteries with red herrings and conflicting testimony** (the user's stated interest): use node design + Three Clue redundancy so red herrings enrich rather than stall; ensure every essential deduction has three independent supports; track which NPCs know/believe/lie about what in the campaign bible.
- **AI-adaptation flag:** the AI's perfect memory is an *advantage* here (it can track a large clue-graph flawlessly) but its over-compliance is a *hazard* (it may hand over the solution too readily or confirm a player's wrong theory sycophantically). Prompt it to reveal core clues reliably but to *withhold interpretation and confirmation*.

### (i) Continuity and record-keeping

- **State is the source of truth, not the model's memory.** (Austin Amento, DEV.) Serialize authoritative game state into every turn and declare it authoritative so the model cannot narrate contradictions (hitting an enemy behind cover, reusing a spent resource). The MCP/campaign-bible tooling setup the user has is exactly the right architecture for this.
- **The context-window continuity failure is real and documented.** (Jørgensen et al., *Static vs. Agentic GM*: v1 "would begin to do worse as the game continued… only the last part of the prompt was utilized, and the first part seemed forgotten." Solo roleplayers have written extensively about manually managing the memory ceiling.) Mitigations: external canon store, structured summaries, re-injection of relevant facts on demand.
- **Inject the right rule/fact exactly when needed.** (Amento's SRD-injection pattern: keyword-extract the player's action, pull the top-N relevant rules into context that turn.) Retrieval beats relying on the model's parametric recall.
- **Keep session logs and a canon tracker; define a retconning policy.** Decide in advance how contradictions are resolved (prefer established canon; use a "script change"-style rewind only with the player's assent — see (k)).
- **AI-adaptation flag:** most human continuity advice exists to compensate for human forgetting; for an AI the failure mode is different (context-window truncation and confabulation), so the fix is architectural (external state) rather than note-taking discipline.

### (j) Session closing and between-session work

- **End on a hook or an unresolved beat.** Close scenes and sessions at a point of tension or decision.
- **Review and carry forward unresolved secrets/clues.** (Sly Flourish's updated practice: bring forward un-uncovered secrets rather than starting fresh each time.)
- **Between sessions, advance the world's offscreen clocks** so the situation the PCs return to has moved.
- **Generate a structured recap/log** as an artifact for the next session's context reload (doubles as the (b) opening recap).

### (k) Tone and safety calibration

- **Lines and veils.** (Ron Edwards, *Sex & Sorcery*.) Lines = content excluded entirely; veils = content allowed but "faded to black." Negotiate before play; default to the most cautious setting.
- **The X-card.** (John Stavropoulos.) Any participant can, with no explanation required, signal to remove/skip current content. For an AI GM, implement an always-available out-of-character command that immediately halts and redirects.
- **Script change.** (Brie Beau Sheldon.) Rewind / pause / fast-forward controls over content and intensity [Golden Lasso Games](https://goldenlassogames.com/pages/safety-tools) [The Wanderer's Tome](https://www.wanderers-tome.com/blog/roleplaying-safety-tools) — a natural fit for an AI, which can literally rewind and re-narrate.
- **Check in and debrief.** Provide explicit out-of-character check-in channels, since the AI cannot read discomfort from cues.
- **AI-adaptation flag:** the entire safety-tools canon assumes humans reading each other; an AI must make these *explicit commands* and cannot rely on noticing distress. This is one place where an AI GM must add scaffolding a human GM gets for free.

### Improv craft (cross-cutting)

- **"Yes, and" — but with the standard GM-specific critique.** (Graham Walmsley, *Play Unsafe*, argues for "be obvious," build on offers, and don't over-plan.) The critique: unconditional "yes-and" degenerates into consequence-free play. Prefer "yes, but / no, and" so contributions have friction. This directly counteracts AI over-compliance — arguably the improv principle *most* worth encoding for an AI GM, precisely because the model's default is unconditional yes.
- **A published AI-GM system prompt** circulating online (Promptsmint's "AI Dungeon Master") explicitly encodes '"yes, and…" or "yes, but…" — never "no" without an alternative' and "player agency is sacred… present situations, not solutions." A useful found example, though it leans into exactly the compliance the research warns about — evaluate it critically rather than adopting wholesale.

### Solo/GM-emulator theory (highly relevant — an AI GM often runs for one player)

- **Mythic Game Master Emulator** (Tana Pigeon): a yes/no oracle (Fate Chart/Fate Check), Chaos Factor (tunes how often the unexpected intrudes), scene structure (expected → check for altered/interrupt), Random Events, Meaning Tables, and tracked Threads/Characters lists. The key insight for AI GMs: solo play needs a *deliberate surprise-injection mechanism* because a single author (or a compliant model) will otherwise collapse into author-stance wish-fulfillment. An oracle roll the model must consult before deciding forces genuine uncertainty.
- **Prevent author-stance collapse.** The core risk in solo/AI play is that the "surprise" disappears because one intelligence controls both sides. Mythic's interrupts and the Czege Principle both target this; an AI GM should have an external randomness source it cannot rationalize away.

### The AI-GM research frontier (Track 1, consolidated with specifics)

- **Over-compliance / "seduced by the narrative."** (Weiying Chen, Junlong Shen, Zhanyuan Guo & Xiaoou Zhou, "Seduced by the Narrative: Assessing Rule Adherence in Semi-Open Textual Sandboxes," arXiv:2607.02802v1, 2 Jul 2026.) Verbatim: "Because LLMs are optimized to maintain textual coherence, they instinctively treat player inputs as creative writing prompts to be accommodated rather than structural game states to be rigorously adjudicated." Their CoC-Seduce benchmark (5,376 samples; generators GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 3.5 Flash; 16 skill categories × 4 world settings; 20 target adjudicator models) found LLMs granting "False Passes" — unearned success on mandatory checks — with failures skewing almost entirely toward leniency rather than excess strictness. Critically: "neither model scale nor explicit reasoning mechanisms reliably confer adjudication robustness, with Pseudo-Logic emerging as the dominant attack vector" (i.e., a player's pseudo-logical justification is the most effective way to bully the model into auto-success; chain-of-thought alone does not fix it). Mitigation direction: robustness to rhetorical manipulation and broader domain knowledge, not just better reasoning.
- **Redirection design.** (Jørgensen et al., "Guiding, Not Railroading," IUI '26, DOI 10.1145/3742413.3789218.) In-world redirection (information, consequences, NPC influence) beats hard denial; measured with the Player Experience Inventory (PXI) and the Artificial Social Agent Questionnaire.
- **Agentic > static prompt.** (Jørgensen, Tharmabalan, Aslan, Hansen & Merritt, "Static Vs. Agentic Game Master AI for Facilitating Solo Role-Playing Experiences," arXiv:2502.19519, presented at ACM CUI 2025, Waterloo.) A multi-agent ReAct system (v2) beat simple prompting (v1) on most PXI constructs — verbatim, "v2 as an agentic system maintains play while significantly improving modularity and game experience, including immersion and curiosity" — with statistically significant gains on mastery, curiosity, immersion, story coherence, and satisfaction. v1's main failures were narrative-coherence drift (numbers of enemies/items changing) and context-window forgetting. They cite Gallotta et al. (2024) for the canonical LLM-GM limitations: difficulty capturing user intent (esp. sarcasm/ambiguity), over-compliance derailing the narrative, and the need to supply world knowledge via prompt engineering (which overflows context in long campaigns).
- **Multi-agent "checker" agents can backfire.** (Minari, Ueno & Lee, "TRPG Game Mastering Using LLM-Based Multi-Agent System," Wordplay Workshop; GPT-4o, Emoklore TRPG, 56 participants.) Adding rules- and scenario-reviewer agents to a GM agent *worsened* rule violations (1.32 → 3.26 average) and spoilers (0.36 → 0.93) and more than doubled response time, because the specialist agents gave incorrect feedback outside their remit ("improper feedback beyond the agent's designated role led to the degradation of otherwise correct responses"). Lesson: more agents ≠ better; strict role scoping and conflict-mediation matter, and a checker that is itself an LLM can degrade correct outputs.
- **CALYPSO** (Andrew Zhu, Lara Martin, Andrew Head, Chris Callison-Burch, "CALYPSO: LLMs as Dungeon Masters' Assistants," Proc. 19th AAAI AIIDE 2023, pp. 380–390, DOI 10.1609/aiide.v19i1.27534): the foundational study positioning LLMs as *co-DMs / assistants* rather than autonomous GMs. Deployed as a Discord bot on Avrae and tested in practice with 71 players; found LLMs good at high-fidelity prose and low-fidelity brainstorming but prone to hallucinating rules and details. A useful baseline caution that autonomous GMing is harder than assistance.
- **Practitioner engineering patterns that work** (Amento, DEV; various GitHub/blog writeups): a versioned system-prompt "contract"; roll-before-outcome enforced and shown as the correct vs. wrong pattern; state serialized and declared authoritative each turn; just-in-time rule injection via retrieval; enforced machine-readable output sections; treating every observed rule-break as a new prompt clause; and front-loading the most-violated rules because rules buried mid-prompt get lost to recency effects.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Encode the anti-sycophancy spine first (highest ROI).** Before anything else, add prompt clauses that: (a) require mechanical resolution *before* narrating any outcome, in a fixed machine-readable block; (b) forbid auto-success and require that failure be possible and consequential; (c) forbid the model from confirming player theories or handing over clue *interpretations*; (d) instruct in-world redirection (information/consequence/NPC), never bare "no," and never unconditional "yes-and." Given the "Seduced by the Narrative" finding that pseudo-logical player justifications are the strongest jailbreak, explicitly instruct the model to adjudicate against game state regardless of how well-argued the player's rationale is. *Benchmark that would change this:* if playtests still show the model letting players auto-win or rubber-stamping wrong theories, escalate to a separate, narrow adjudication call the narrative model cannot override (note: keep it single and tightly scoped — see Stage 6).

**Stage 2 — Make state external and authoritative.** Use the MCP/campaign-bible tooling to serialize game state into every turn and declare it the source of truth; implement retrieval that injects the relevant rules/canon for the current action. Store the mystery as an explicit node/clue graph (Alexandrian) with a revelation list, and enforce GUMSHOE-style reliable delivery of *core* clues plus Three-Clue redundancy. *Benchmark:* if continuity errors (changed item counts, forgotten NPCs, contradicted canon) appear as sessions lengthen, tighten summarization and re-injection rather than enlarging the raw context.

**Stage 3 — Choose your stances on the four conflict axes, explicitly, in the prompt.** (1) Prep vs. improv; (2) fan-of-characters vs. neutral referee; (3) fail-forward vs. lethal consequence; (4) how much information is player-facing vs. GM-facing. Write the chosen stance as an explicit directive; do not let the model average them. Given the user's taste for complex mysteries, a defensible default is: moderately prep-heavy on the clue graph, lean neutral-referee (to fight sycophancy), fail-forward on investigation but real consequences on physical danger, and strict GM-facing secrecy on solutions.

**Stage 4 — Add a deliberate surprise engine for solo play.** Give the AI an oracle/random source (Mythic-style yes/no + chaos factor + occasional random event/scene interrupt) that it must consult and cannot rationalize away, to prevent author-stance collapse and inject genuine uncertainty a compliant model won't generate.

**Stage 5 — Layer the prep and pacing checklists as operating procedure.** Bake the Lazy DM eight-step prep into a pre-session routine; bake hope/fear beat variation and "cut to the interesting part" into scene handling; bake lines/veils + an always-on X-card command into every session. *Benchmark:* if the single player reports flat pacing or tension collapse, add explicit beat-direction tracking and stakes-before-roll enforcement.

**Stage 6 — Be cautious with multi-agent elaboration.** The evidence is mixed-to-negative: reviewer agents worsened correctness (rule violations 1.32 → 3.26) and doubled latency in the Minari et al. study, even as the agentic ReAct architecture in Jørgensen et al. improved experience. The reconciling lesson: agentic *tool use and state management* help, but LLM *committees that critique each other* can hurt. Prefer a strong single GM prompt with tool-grounded state and at most one narrow, well-scoped adjudication check; only add agents where you can prove they help.

## Caveats

- **The AI-GM literature is young, small, and fast-moving.** Sample sizes are small (the Static-vs-Agentic study ran 8 and 12 participants; the multi-agent TRPG study 56), several key results are single-study, and one closely relevant paper ("Seduced by the Narrative") is a very recent preprint referencing not-yet-widely-released models — treat its specific numbers as provisional. The *direction* of findings (over-compliance is the core problem; in-world redirection beats denial; external state beats parametric memory; scale and chain-of-thought alone don't fix adjudication) is corroborated across multiple sources and is high-confidence; specific magnitudes are not.
- **Much of the human canon is one practitioner's opinion, not empirical fact.** The Alexandrian, Sly Flourish, Robin Laws, Walmsley, and the Principia authors are influential and battle-tested at real tables, but their advice is craft wisdom, not research. Where a claim is broadly held across traditions (e.g., "only roll when the outcome is uncertain and interesting") it is noted as such; where it is a specific author's framework (e.g., Three Clue Rule, beat analysis) it is attributed so you can weight it.
- **The traditions genuinely conflict; this document deliberately does not resolve those conflicts for you.** Blending them produces incoherent play. The Recommendations take positions, but they are defaults for this user's stated preferences, not universal truths.
- **Transfer hazards are flagged inline but worth restating:** any advice premised on reading body language, on human memory limits, or on physical table presence needs rethinking or replacing for an AI GM, and the safety-tools canon in particular must be re-implemented as explicit commands rather than social cues.
